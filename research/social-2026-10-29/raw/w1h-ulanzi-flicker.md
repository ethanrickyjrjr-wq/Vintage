# Eliminating Flickering on High-Speed Slow Motion Video with Compact LED Panels
[![Chloe Miller](https://www.ulanzi.com/cdn/shop/files/Chloe_Miller.png?v=1776844895&width=72) ](https://www.ulanzi.com/pages/authors/chloe-miller)By[Chloe Miller](https://www.ulanzi.com/pages/authors/chloe-miller) September 15, 2026 [ 0 comments ](https://www.ulanzi.com/blogs/knowledges/why-led-panels-flicker-120fps-240fps#comments) Reviewed by[Chloe Miller](https://www.ulanzi.com/pages/authors/chloe-miller)
Understand how PWM waveform, shutter speed, and dimming interact at 120fps and 240fps, and follow an ordered pre-shoot test routine to eliminate banding.

A compact LED video light flickers at 120fps or 240fps when the camera samples a light output that is changing faster than the eye can see. The light itself may use pulse-width modulation (PWM) to control brightness, and the exact relationship between that pulsing waveform, your frame rate, and your shutter timing decides whether the footage shows bands. There is no single frequency number that guarantees clean footage at every setting, so the reliable fix is to test your exact camera and light setup and only keep footage that passes.
## Why Compact LED Panels Flicker at 120fps and 240fps
Flicker in your footage comes from uneven exposure between frames, not from a light that is simply "bad." It happens when your camera's shutter captures a different slice of a changing light output from one frame to the next.
### The light is not necessarily steady over time
Many compact LED panels use PWM to control brightness. Instead of just lowering the current, the driver switches the LED between full current and zero current many times per second, and the eye blends this into what looks like a steady, dimmed light. The [DOE flicker research](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/ssl/flicker-research) points out that drivers can produce this switching in an unlimited variety of waveform shapes, modulation depths, and duty cycles, not just at a single fixed frequency. That matters because a light's stated PWM frequency describes only one property. Two panels with the same frequency label can still behave differently depending on how deep the modulation is and how the waveform is shaped, so the frequency number by itself does not tell you how the light will look on camera.
### Why 240fps is more demanding than normal-speed capture
A higher frame rate gives the camera less time to sample the light. At 120fps, each frame covers roughly 8.33 milliseconds; at 240fps, that shrinks to about 4.17 milliseconds. As [RED's flicker-free video guide](https://www.reddigitalcinema.com/red-101/flicker-free-video-tutorial) explains, flickering results when the combination of shutter speed and frame rate captures a different fraction of the light's pulses in each frame. Shorter frame intervals make it more likely that consecutive frames land on different points in the waveform, which is why a light that looks fine at 24fps or 30fps can still show bands once you push into slow motion.
## How Frame Rate, Shutter Speed, and Dimming Change the Result
Frame rate, shutter timing, and brightness are three separate variables that each change how the camera samples the light. Passing at one combination does not guarantee a pass at another.
### At 120fps, start with the planned shutter
Test the exact shutter speed or shutter angle you plan to use, not just the frame rate. Shutter timing sets how long each frame's exposure window stays open, which determines how much of the light's waveform gets captured. Run this test at the actual brightness level you intend to shoot at, because a result at full output does not confirm behavior at a lower setting. Treat a clean pass at 120fps as evidence for that specific frame rate, shutter, and brightness combination only.
![Rhythmic bands of changing light cross a dark interior, evoking flicker revealed during slow-motion capture.](https://cdn.shopify.com/s/files/1/0136/3119/3188/files/B_why_led_panels_flicker_120fps_240fps_cover.png?v=1789461922)
### At 240fps, repeat the test from scratch
Do not assume a panel that passed at 120fps will automatically pass at 240fps. The shorter 4.17-millisecond frame interval can expose waveform variation that a slower frame rate or the naked eye never revealed. Keep your camera settings, brightness level, power source, and any surrounding lights fixed, then change only the frame rate, so you can tell whether 240fps itself is the variable causing the problem.
### Dimming creates another operating condition
A panel that looks clean at full brightness can start banding once you dim it. According to a [manufacturer technical explanation of PWM behavior](https://insights.acuitybrands.com/eldoled-blog/article-flicker-performance-in-led-lighting), PWM dimming works by cycling the LED between full current and zero current at a fixed rate, and how that cycling behaves can shift as the duty cycle changes. If your shoot includes battery power, USB-C PD, or a DC adapter, factor that into the test too, since the power source can be part of the operating condition. A dimming percentage on a control app is not itself a PWM measurement, so treat every new brightness level as its own test.
## Troubleshoot Bands, Flicker, and Dimmed Output Before the Shoot
When footage shows bands or flicker, work through a fixed order of checks instead of guessing at a fix. This isolates the cause and gives you a pass/fail answer before you're on set.
### Use this order when footage shows flicker or bands
  1. Set your camera to the exact frame rate, shutter speed or angle, and exposure settings you plan to use on the real shoot.
  2. Set the LED panel to the exact brightness and power condition (battery, DC, or USB-C PD) you plan to use.
  3. Turn off or physically block any other artificial lights so you are only testing the panel in question.
  4. Record a short clip and review it at full resolution, ideally on the delivery workflow you'll actually use.
  5. Change only one variable at a time, brightness, shutter, or frame rate, then retest before accepting the new configuration.


This same discipline applies before any shoot with a new setup; our [mobile rig test checklist](https://www.ulanzi.com/blogs/knowledges/mobile-rig-test-checklist-before-a-real-shoot) covers the broader pre-shoot steps for framing, audio, and power alongside lighting.
### Interpret the result without overdiagnosing
If bands disappear after you adjust the shutter, that confirms a timing interaction with the light's waveform, but it does not prove the panel is flicker-free at every other setting. If bands only appear after dimming, the operating condition changed, which points to the driver's behavior at that brightness rather than a defective unit. If bands vanish once you remove other artificial lights, the issue may belong to mixed lighting rather than the compact panel itself.
## What to Verify Before Choosing a Compact LED Panel
Choose a panel based on measured evidence at your actual settings, not a marketing label. A standard PWM-dimmed panel and a panel marketed as high-frequency or flicker-controlled both need to prove themselves at your frame rate, shutter, and brightness before you trust them for 120fps or 240fps work.
### Compare the evidence, not the marketing label  
| What to check  | Standard PWM-dimmed panel  | High-frequency or verified flicker-controlled panel  |  
| --- | --- | --- |  
| Waveform evidence  | Often undocumented; frequency label only  | May include measured waveform or flicker-percentage data  |  
| Frequency claim  | Stated frequency alone, no context  | Frequency plus operating-condition detail is more useful  |  
| Dimming coverage  | Behavior at each brightness usually untested  | Documentation may cover multiple brightness levels  |  
| 120fps/240fps footage coverage  | Rarely provided  | Occasionally provided, but not guaranteed  |  
| Choice condition  | Usable only if it passes your exact-setting test  | Preferred when measurements exist, but still needs your camera test  |  
A stated frequency above 12,000 Hz is sometimes marketed as a safer choice, but the DOE's flicker research is clear that no single metric predicts every application. The [IEEE 1789-2015 standard](https://standards.ieee.org/standard/1789-2015.html) is useful background for modulation terminology and general flicker-risk guidance, but it is not a camera-flicker certification and does not set a 120fps or 240fps threshold. Treat any frequency number as a starting point for further evidence, not a pass.
### Minimum evidence to request
Ask for waveform or flicker-percentage measurements, not just a frequency label. Confirm the brightness levels and control range that were actually tested, since dimming can change driver behavior. Ask whether the manufacturer has footage or measurements at your planned frame rate and shutter setting, since that is the closest match to your real use case. Compact size, wattage, CRI rating, and dimming range describe other qualities of a light, but none of them establish flicker performance at high frame rates. Our supplied compact panels document brightness controls and physical dimensions, but that information does not include PWM frequency or high-speed-camera flicker measurements, so we can't call any of them verified flicker-free for 120fps or 240fps use; the exact-setting test above remains the deciding factor. If you're browsing options, our [LED panel lights](https://www.ulanzi.com/collections/led-panel-lights) collection is a starting point for size and control features, though it is not a substitute for your own camera test.

## FAQs
### Is a high PWM frequency number enough to guarantee flicker-free 120fps or 240fps footage?
No single frequency number guarantees a clean result. Frequency describes only one property of the light's waveform; modulation depth, duty cycle, and the exact shutter and frame rate you use all affect whether a frame captures uneven exposure. Treat any frequency claim as a starting point, then confirm with a recorded test at your actual settings.
### Why does dimming a panel introduce flicker that wasn't there at full brightness?
Dimming through PWM changes how the driver cycles the LED between full and zero current, which can alter the waveform your camera samples. A panel that passes at full output is being tested under a different operating condition than the same panel dimmed to 30 percent, so each brightness level needs its own check.
### Do I need to test 120fps and 240fps separately, or does passing one confirm the other?
Test them separately. The frame interval at 240fps is roughly half that of 120fps, which gives the camera less time to sample the light and can reveal waveform variation that a slower frame rate hides. A pass at 120fps is evidence for that frame rate alone.
### What should I ask a seller for beyond a "flicker-free" claim?
Ask for measured waveform or flicker-percentage data tied to specific brightness levels, not just a marketing label. Ask whether that data, or actual footage, covers the frame rate and shutter setting you plan to shoot with. Without that, treat the claim as unverified for your use case and rely on your own recorded test.
