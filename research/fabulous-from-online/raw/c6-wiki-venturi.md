**Robert Charles Venturi Jr.** (June 25, 1925 – September 18, 2018) was an American architect, founding principal of the firm Venturi, Scott Brown and Associates.
Together with his wife and partner, [Denise Scott Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denise_Scott_Brown "Denise Scott Brown"), he helped shape the way that architects, planners and students experience and think about architecture and the built environment. Their buildings, planning, theoretical writings, and teaching have also contributed to the expansion of discourse about architecture.
Venturi was awarded the [Pritzker Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pritzker_Prize "Pritzker Prize") in Architecture in 1991; the prize was awarded to him alone, despite a request to include his equal partner, Scott Brown. Subsequently, a group of women architects attempted to get her name added retroactively to the prize, but the Pritzker Prize jury declined to do so.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Venturi#cite_note-1)[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Venturi#cite_note-2)[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Venturi#cite_note-3) Venturi coined the maxim "Less is a bore", a [postmodern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postmodern_architecture "Postmodern architecture") antidote to [Mies van der Rohe's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Mies_van_der_Rohe "Ludwig Mies van der Rohe") famous [modernist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernism "Modernism") dictum "Less is more". Venturi lived in [Philadelphia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia "Philadelphia") with Denise Scott Brown. He is the father of James Venturi, founder and principal of ReThink Studio.
## Early life and education
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Robert_Venturi&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Early life and education")]
Venturi was born in [Philadelphia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia "Philadelphia") to Robert Venturi Sr. and Vanna (née Luizi) Venturi, and was raised as a [Quaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quakers "Quakers").[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Venturi#cite_note-yearbook-4) Venturi attended school at the [Episcopal Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Episcopal_Academy "Episcopal Academy") in [Merion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merion "Merion"), [Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pennsylvania "Pennsylvania").[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Venturi#cite_note-5) He graduated _[summa cum laude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summa_cum_laude "Summa cum laude")_ from [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University "Princeton University") in 1947 where he was a member-elect of [Phi Beta Kappa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phi_Beta_Kappa "Phi Beta Kappa") and won the D'Amato Prize in Architecture.[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Venturi#cite_note-yearbook-4) He received his [M.F.A.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Fine_Arts "Master of Fine Arts") from Princeton in 1950. The educational program at Princeton under Professor [Jean Labatut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Labatut_\(architect\) "Jean Labatut \(architect\)"), who offered provocative design studios within a Beaux-Arts pedagogical framework,[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Venturi#cite_note-6) was a key factor in Venturi's development of an approach to [architectural theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architectural_theory "Architectural theory") and design that drew from architectural history and commercial architecture in analytical, as opposed to stylistic, terms.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Venturi#cite_note-PritzkerPrize-7) In 1951 he briefly worked under [Eero Saarinen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eero_Saarinen "Eero Saarinen") in [Bloomfield Hills, Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloomfield_Hills,_Michigan "Bloomfield Hills, Michigan"), and later for [Louis Kahn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Kahn "Louis Kahn") in Philadelphia. He was awarded the [Rome Prize Fellowship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rome_Prize_Fellowship "Rome Prize Fellowship") at the [American Academy in Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_in_Rome "American Academy in Rome") in 1954, where he studied and toured Europe for two years.
From 1959 to 1967, Venturi held teaching positions at the [University of Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Pennsylvania "University of Pennsylvania"), where he served as Kahn's teaching assistant, an instructor, and later, as associate professor. It was there, in 1960, that he met fellow faculty member, architect and planner [Denise Scott Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denise_Scott_Brown "Denise Scott Brown"). Venturi taught later at the [Yale School of Architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_School_of_Architecture "Yale School of Architecture") and was a visiting lecturer with Scott Brown in 2003 at the [Graduate School of Design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_Graduate_School_of_Design "Harvard Graduate School of Design") at [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University").
## Career
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Robert_Venturi&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Career")]
[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b9/Denise_Scott_Brown_1978_%C2%A9_Lynn_Gilbert.jpg/250px-Denise_Scott_Brown_1978_%C2%A9_Lynn_Gilbert.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Denise_Scott_Brown_1978_%C2%A9_Lynn_Gilbert.jpg)Venturi's wife and business partner Denise Scott Brown, 1978
A controversial critic of what he saw as the blithely [functionalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functionalism_\(architecture\) "Functionalism \(architecture\)") and symbolically vacuous architecture of corporate modernism during the 1950s, Venturi was one of the first architects to question some of the premises of the Modern Movement. He published his "gentle manifesto", _[Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complexity_and_Contradiction_in_Architecture "Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture")_ in 1966; in its introduction, [Vincent Scully](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincent_Scully "Vincent Scully") called it "probably the most important writing on the making of architecture since [Le Corbusier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Corbusier "Le Corbusier")'s [_Vers Une Architecture_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Towards_a_New_Architecture "Towards a New Architecture") of 1923." The work was derived from course lectures at the University of Pennsylvania, and Venturi received a grant from the [Graham Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graham_Foundation "Graham Foundation") in 1965 to aid in its completion. The book demonstrated, through countless examples, an approach to understanding architectural composition and complexity, and the resulting richness and interest. Citing [vernacular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vernacular_architecture "Vernacular architecture") as well as high-style sources, Venturi drew new lessons from the buildings of architects familiar ([Michelangelo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelangelo "Michelangelo"), [Alvar Aalto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alvar_Aalto "Alvar Aalto")) and, at the time, forgotten ([Frank Furness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Furness "Frank Furness"), [Edwin Lutyens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Lutyens "Edwin Lutyens")). He made a case for "the difficult whole" rather than the diagrammatic forms popular at the time, and included examples — both built and unrealized — of his work to demonstrate the possible application of such techniques. The book has been published in 18 languages to date.[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Venturi#cite_note-8)
Immediately hailed as a theorist and designer with radical ideas, Venturi went to teach a series of studios at the [Yale School of Architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_School_of_Architecture "Yale School of Architecture") in the mid-1960s. The most famous of these was a studio in 1968 in which Venturi and Scott Brown, together with [Steven Izenour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steven_Izenour "Steven Izenour"), led a team of students to document and analyze the [Las Vegas Strip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Las_Vegas_Strip "Las Vegas Strip"), perhaps the least likely subject for a serious research project imaginable.[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Venturi#cite_note-9) In 1972, Venturi, Scott Brown and Izenour published the folio, _A Significance for A &P Parking Lots, or [Learning from Las Vegas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Learning_from_Las_Vegas "Learning from Las Vegas")._ It was revised using the student work as a foil for new theory, and reissued in 1977 as _Learning from Las Vegas: the Forgotten Symbolism of Architectural Form_. This second manifesto was an even more stinging rebuke to orthodox modernism and elite architectural tastes. The book coined the terms "Duck" and "Decorated Shed", descriptions of the two predominant ways of embodying iconography in buildings. The work of Venturi, Scott Brown, and [John Rauch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Rauch_\(architect\) "John Rauch \(architect\)")[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Venturi#cite_note-10) adopted the latter strategy, producing formally simple "decorated sheds" with rich, complex, and often shocking ornamental flourishes. Venturi and his wife co-wrote several more books at the end of the century, but these two have so far proved to be the most influential.[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Venturi#cite_note-11)
## Architecture
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Robert_Venturi&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Architecture")]
[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e7/Guildhouse_Philly.JPG/250px-Guildhouse_Philly.JPG?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Guildhouse_Philly.JPG)The [Guild House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guild_House_\(Philadelphia\) "Guild House \(Philadelphia\)"), completed 1964, on Spring Garden Street in [Philadelphia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia "Philadelphia") [![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/ba/Espicopal_Acad_int.JPG/250px-Espicopal_Acad_int.JPG?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Espicopal_Acad_int.JPG)Chapel at the Episcopal Academy in [Newtown Square, Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newtown_Square,_Pennsylvania "Newtown Square, Pennsylvania") (2010)
The architecture of Robert Venturi, although perhaps not as familiar today as his books, helped redirect American architecture away from a widely practiced modernism in the 1960s to a more exploratory design approach that openly drew lessons from architectural history and responded to the everyday context of the American city.[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Venturi#cite_note-12) Venturi's buildings typically juxtapose architectural systems, elements and aims, to acknowledge the conflicts often inherent in a project or site. This "inclusive" approach contrasted with the typical modernist effort to resolve and unify all factors in a complete and rigidly structured—and possibly less functional and more simplistic—work of art. The diverse range of buildings of Venturi's early career offered surprising alternatives to then current architectural practice, with "impure" forms (such as the North Penn Visiting Nurses Headquarters), apparently casual asymmetries (as at the Vanna Venturi House), and pop-style [supergraphics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supergraphics "Supergraphics") and geometries (for instance, the Lieb House).
Venturi created the firm Venturi and Short with William Short in 1960. In his architectural design Venturi was influenced by early masters such as [Michelangelo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelangelo "Michelangelo") and [Palladio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrea_Palladio "Andrea Palladio"), and modern masters including [Le Corbusier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Corbusier "Le Corbusier"), [Alvar Aalto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alvar_Aalto "Alvar Aalto"), [Louis Kahn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Kahn "Louis Kahn") and [Eero Saarinen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eero_Saarinen "Eero Saarinen").[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Venturi#cite_note-13) After John Rauch replaced Short as partner in 1964, the firm's name changed to Venturi and Rauch. Venturi married [Denise Scott Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denise_Scott_Brown "Denise Scott Brown") on July 23, 1967, in [Santa Monica, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Monica,_California "Santa Monica, California"), and in 1969, Scott Brown joined the firm as partner in charge of planning. In 1980, The firm's name became Venturi, Rauch, and Scott Brown, and after Rauch's resignation in 1989, Venturi, Scott Brown, and Associates. The firm, based in [Manayunk, Philadelphia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manayunk,_Philadelphia "Manayunk, Philadelphia"), was awarded the Architecture Firm Award by the [American Institute of Architects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Institute_of_Architects "American Institute of Architects") in 1985. The practice's recent work includes many commissions from academic institutions, including campus planning and university buildings, and civic buildings in London, [Toulouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toulouse "Toulouse"), and Japan.
Venturi's architecture has had worldwide influence, beginning in the late 1960s with the dissemination of the broken-gable roof of the Vanna Venturi House and the segmentally arched window and interrupted string courses of Guild House. The playful variations on vernacular house types seen in the Trubeck and Wislocki Houses offered a new way to embrace, but transform, familiar forms. The facade patterning of the [Allen Memorial Art Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allen_Memorial_Art_Museum "Allen Memorial Art Museum") in Oberlin and the laboratory buildings demonstrated a treatment of the vertical surfaces of buildings that is both decorative and abstract, drawing from vernacular and historic architecture while still being modern. Venturi's work arguably provided a key influence at important times in the careers of architects [Robert A. M. Stern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_A._M._Stern "Robert A. M. Stern"), [Rem Koolhaas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rem_Koolhaas "Rem Koolhaas"), [Philip Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Johnson "Philip Johnson"), [Michael Graves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Graves "Michael Graves"), [Graham Gund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graham_Gund "Graham Gund") and [James Stirling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Stirling_\(architect\) "James Stirling \(architect\)"), among others.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
Venturi was a Fellow of the [American Academy in Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_in_Rome "American Academy in Rome"), the [American Institute of Architects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Institute_of_Architects "American Institute of Architects"), [The American Academy of Arts and Letters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Letters "The American Academy of Arts and Letters") and an Honorary Fellow of the [Royal Institute of British Architects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Institute_of_British_Architects "Royal Institute of British Architects").
## Death
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Robert_Venturi&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Death")]
Venturi died on September 18, 2018, in [Philadelphia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia "Philadelphia") from complications of [Alzheimer's disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alzheimer's_disease "Alzheimer's disease").[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Venturi#cite_note-14)[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Venturi#cite_note-15) He was 93.
In the wake of Venturi's death, [Michael Kimmelman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Kimmelman "Michael Kimmelman"), the current architecture critic for _[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times "The New York Times")_ , tweeted..."RIP the great, inspiring Robert Venturi who opened millions of eyes and whole new ways of thinking about the richness of our architectural environment, and whose diverse work with Denise Scott Brown contains a mix of wit and humanity that continues to transcend labels and time".[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Venturi#cite_note-16)[_[bettersourceneeded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability#Questionable_sources "Wikipedia:Verifiability")_]
