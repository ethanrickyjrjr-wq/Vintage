# HOA Vendor Insurance Requirements in Florida
By [Ralph Ceballos](https://www.prestigeinsurance.com/blog/author/ralphprestigeinsurance-com/)May 19, 2026October 21st, 2026[No Comments](https://www.prestigeinsurance.com/blog/hoa-vendor-insurance-requirements-in-florida/#respond)
[Home](https://www.prestigeinsurance.com/) » HOA Vendor Insurance Requirements in Florida
![](https://b4179059.assetcdn.net/2.0/4179059/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/0439d75c-8728-4025-b502-66f757050cd6.png?lossy=2&strip=1&webp=1)
A landscaper’s employee falls from a ladder trimming a palm on common property. The vendor’s certificate showed general liability and, in the [workers’ compensation](https://www.prestigeinsurance.com/business-insurance/workers-compensation-insurance/) box, an exemption.
The worker has medical bills and no workers’ compensation benefits. His attorney looks for the next available party, and the next available party is the association whose property he was standing on.
The certificate was accurate. The board collected it, filed it, and never asked what the exemption meant.
## Start With the Work, Not the Certificate
The most common failure in vendor management is running the process backward. Hire the contractor, request a certificate, confirm general liability appears somewhere on it, file it, assume the risk is handled.
A real process runs the other direction. Scope of work, then contract, then insurance requirements sized to that work, then the certificate, then the endorsements that actually create rights, then verification, then ongoing monitoring.
The first question is not whether the vendor has insurance. It is what the association is hiring this company to do and what could realistically go wrong doing it. A landscaper cutting grass and a roofer opening a building envelope in September are not the same risk, and applying one checklist to both produces the appearance of risk management without the substance.
## A Certificate Is Evidence, Not the Policy
This is the distinction every board and manager should internalize.
A certificate is an administrative summary. Carrier, policy numbers, dates, limits. It is not the policy, and seeing a million dollars of general liability on it reveals nothing about whether the policy excludes the work being performed, whether the classification matches the operation, or whether the endorsements anyone required were ever actually added.
The frequent and costly version of this: **a certificate listing the association as certificate holder does not make the association an additional insured.** A certificate holder receives evidence that a policy exists. An additional insured receives actual rights under the vendor’s policy, and only the endorsement creates that. Where additional insured status matters, ask for the endorsement itself.
Three related terms get used interchangeably and mean different things. **Indemnification** is a contractual obligation between the parties, separate from any insurance right. **Primary and noncontributory** addresses how the vendor’s coverage responds relative to the association’s own. **Waiver of subrogation** limits the vendor’s insurer from pursuing the association after paying a claim. A well-built requirement uses the ones that fit the relationship rather than all of them reflexively.
## Two Additional Insured Details That Decide Claims
**Ongoing operations or completed operations.** Additional insured endorsements come in both forms. An ongoing operations endorsement protects the association while the work is being performed and stops when the work is complete. A completed operations endorsement continues afterward.
That distinction is the whole ballgame on construction work, because contractor losses most often surface after the job is finished. A roof repair that allows water intrusion three months later, discovered during the next heavy rain, falls outside an ongoing-operations-only endorsement. The association holding that certificate believes it has protection it no longer has.
**Blanket endorsements conditioned on a written contract.** Many vendors carry blanket additional insured wording that grants status only where a written contract requires it. If the association never executed a written agreement, the blanket endorsement grants nothing. The endorsement is real and the association is not an additional insured.
## The Workers’ Compensation Exemption Problem
Florida allows certain owners and officers to file exemptions from workers’ compensation coverage, and small vendors use them routinely.
The consequence for the association is direct. If a worker is injured on association property and no workers’ compensation policy responds, that worker’s recovery has to come from somewhere, and the property owner is the obvious candidate. Workers’ compensation on a vendor’s policy is not only protecting the vendor’s employees. It is the thing that keeps their injuries from becoming the association’s liability claim.
Boards should treat a workers’ compensation exemption on a certificate as a question rather than a line item, particularly for any vendor whose people climb, lift, or work at height. It is also worth confirming that the coverage shown covers the people actually on site, not just an owner who never leaves the truck.
## Scale the Review to the Risk
A five thousand dollar landscaping contract and a multimillion dollar roofing project should not receive identical scrutiny.
A materially cheaper proposal deserves a real question about why. Narrower scope, less experienced crews, or thinner insurance all hide behind an attractive number. Licensing matters separately from insurance, since a contractor can carry a policy and still be unqualified or improperly licensed for regulated work.
**Roofers** compromise the building’s weather protection during the least predictable months of the year, and a storm over an open roof can reach multiple units before anyone responds. **Plumbers and electricians** create damage far from their work area, since a failed connection upstairs reaches units several floors down. **Tree contractors** work directly over buildings, vehicles, and people. **Security vendors** carry assault, battery, and improper detention exposures that standard general liability frequently excludes without specific endorsements. **Pool and pest vendors** handle chemicals, which raises a pollution question most certificates do not address. **Engineers, architects, and accountants** create professional judgment exposure that general liability was never designed to cover, which makes professional liability the relevant question instead of the limit on the certificate.
## Limits, Aggregates, and Umbrellas
A limit that satisfies a template may not fit the project.
A vendor’s general aggregate applies across its entire book of business, not to your association alone. A company working forty properties carries one aggregate, and losses elsewhere can erode what remains available. On a significant contract it is fair to ask whether a per-project aggregate endorsement is in place.
Where an [umbrella](https://www.prestigeinsurance.com/business-insurance/commercial-umbrella-insurance/) is required, confirm it follows form over the coverages that matter. An umbrella that excludes what the association was most concerned about adds a number and nothing else.
## Subcontractors Are Where the Chain Breaks
A board can carefully review a general contractor’s insurance and never see the roofing, electrical, or concrete subcontractors doing the actual work.
The contract should state whether subcontracting is permitted, who verifies subcontractor insurance, and how the requirement flows down. Establishing this before bidding means every contractor prices it in, which works considerably better than discovering after an uninsured subcontractor caused a loss that nobody ever checked.
## Coverage Does Not End When the Invoice Does
The most serious contractor losses surface after the work looks complete. A plumbing connection failing months later. Roofing work allowing intrusion that appears at the next heavy rain.
That is why completed operations coverage matters on construction-type projects, and why the additional insured form discussed above matters alongside it.
Certificates also expire mid-project. A vendor relationship that satisfied every requirement at signing can lapse six months in if nobody tracks renewal dates. Cancellation notice is worth addressing in the contract too, since certificates generally do not obligate the carrier to notify the association of anything. Requiring the vendor to provide notice is more reliable than assuming someone else will.
## This Is Also a Governance Decision
A board that reviewed qualified contractors, documented the decision, and verified appropriate requirements tells a very different story than a board that hired informally and cannot locate the paperwork afterward.
If a serious vendor loss occurs, owners will ask who selected the contractor, whether licensing was verified, and whether the board followed a reasonable process. Those answers separate a defensible decision from a governance exposure.
## What Boards Should Verify
  * Is the association an additional insured by endorsement, not just a certificate holder?
  * Is the endorsement ongoing operations, completed operations, or both?
  * If the endorsement is blanket and conditioned on a written contract, is there a written contract?
  * Does the vendor carry workers’ compensation, or is an exemption filed, and does the coverage reach the people actually on site?
  * Is the vendor properly licensed for the regulated work being performed?
  * Does the general liability exclude anything central to this work?
  * Is the vendor’s aggregate shared across its whole book, or endorsed per project?
  * If an umbrella is required, does it follow form?
  * Does the contract address subcontracting and flow-down of insurance requirements?
  * Is professional liability required where the vendor exercises professional judgment?
  * Who tracks expiration dates, and what happens if coverage lapses mid-project?
