Completion of filming
"That's a wrap!" redirects here; not to be confused with [It's a Wrap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/It's_a_Wrap_\(disambiguation\) "It's a Wrap \(disambiguation\)").
**Wrap** , as used in the phrase "**That's a wrap** ", has been used by [directors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_director "Film director") since the early days of the [film industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_industry "Film industry") to signal the end of filming. Since [the 1920s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_cinema_in_the_United_States#Post_1920s_the_modern_era "History of cinema in the United States"), filmmakers have been using this phrase when [principal photography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_photography "Principal photography") is concluded and the film is ready to go into [post-production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-production "Post-production").[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wrap_\(filmmaking\)#cite_note-1)[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wrap_\(filmmaking\)#cite_note-2) At that point, it is traditional to hold a **wrap party** for the cast and crew of the film.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wrap_\(filmmaking\)#cite_note-3)[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wrap_\(filmmaking\)#cite_note-4) This marks the end of the actors' collaboration with each other, the director, [producer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_producer "Film producer"), and [crew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_crew "Film crew") on the film (except for possible [dubbing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dubbing "Dubbing") or [pick-ups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pick-up_\(filmmaking\) "Pick-up \(filmmaking\)")). However, the [leading cast members](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leading_actor "Leading actor") may be called in several months or years later to help [promote the film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_promotion "Film promotion") when it is ready to be released.
The long-commonplace phrase "Let's wrap it up" or "Time to wrap things up" is the most likely origin. The term "wrap" is sometimes said to be an acronym for "wind, reel, and print”. Printing would typically be done at the end of each shoot day (or when else desired, usually as soon as possible for safety of the picture) and not at the end of an entire shoot. This is illustrated by use of the phrase "cut and print", which was used to signify a request the film just shot should be "printed" (the negative developed and transferred to a positive print) for viewing.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wrap_\(filmmaking\)#cite_note-5) Film is neither wound nor reeled at the end of a shoot but rather unloaded and "canned", which is where "It's in the can" originates. 
"That's a wrap!" can also be heard in similar contexts such as [photo shoots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photo_shoots "Photo shoots").
Some directors also announce "wraps" for each actor when their work concludes (e.g., "That's a wrap for John Doe").
