## 0–9
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: 0–9")] 

[180-degree rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/180-degree_rule "180-degree rule")
    A basic guideline regarding the on-screen spatial relationship between a character and another character or object within a scene. By keeping the camera on one side of an imaginary axis between two characters, the first character is always [frame right](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#screen_direction) of the second character. Moving the camera over the axis is called _jumping the line_ or _crossing the line_ ; breaking the 180-degree rule by shooting on all sides is known as [shooting in the round](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#shooting_in_the_round).[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cite_note-1) 

[30-degree rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/30-degree_rule "30-degree rule")
    A basic [film editing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#editing) guideline that states the camera should move at least 30 degrees relative to the subject between successive shots of the same subject. If the camera moves less than 30 degrees, the transition between shots may look like a [jump cut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#jump_cut), which could jar the audience and take them out of the story by causing them to focus on the film technique rather than the narrative itself.[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cite_note-hollywoodlexicon-2) 

[3D film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3D_film "3D film")

Also called a **three-dimensional film** , **three-dimensional stereoscopic film** , or **S3D film**.
    A type of [motion picture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#motion_picture) that utilizes special filming techniques to create the illusion of [depth perception](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Depth_perception "Depth perception"), hence adding a [third dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_dimension "Third dimension").[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cite_note-3)

## A
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: A")] 

[A roll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_roll "A roll")


[Abby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martini_Shot "Martini Shot")
    "The Abby" is the second-to-last shot. Named after assistant director, [Abner E "Abby" Singer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abby_Singer "Abby Singer"), who called out the shot to give the crew extra time to prepare for a company move or wrap.[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cite_note-4) 

[above-the-line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Above-the-line_\(filmmaking\) "Above-the-line \(filmmaking\)")
    The list of individuals who guide and influence the creative direction, process, and voice of a given narrative in a film and related expenditures. These roles include but are not limited to the [screenwriter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#screenwriter), [producer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#producer), [director](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#director), and [actors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#actor). Contrast _[below-the-line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#below-the-line)_.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cite_note-5) 

accelerated montage


accent light


[acousmatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acousmatic "Acousmatic")
    Sound that is heard without an originating cause being seen. 

action axis


[actor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actor "Actor")

A female actor is called an **actress**.
    Any person, male or female, who portrays a [character](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#character) in a performance.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cite_note-6) 

[adaptation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_adaptation "Film adaptation")
    The transfer of a creative work or story, fiction or nonfiction, whole or in part, to a motion picture format; i.e. the reimagining or rewriting of an originally non-film work with the specific intention of presenting it in the form of a film. 

[aerial perspective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerial_perspective "Aerial perspective")


[aerial shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerial_shot "Aerial shot")


alternate ending


[ambient light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Available_light "Available light")

Also called **available light**.
    Any source of light that is not explicitly supplied by the [cinematographer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cinematographer). The term usually refers to sources of light that are already "available" naturally (e.g. the Sun, Moon, lightning) or artificial light that is already being used (e.g. to light a room).[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cite_note-7) 

[American night](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Day_for_night "Day for night")


[American shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_shot "American shot")

Also called a **3/4 shot**.
    A translation of a phrase from [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France "France") [film criticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#film_criticism), _plan américain_ , which refers to a medium-long ("knee") [film shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#shot) of a group of characters, who are arranged so that all are visible to the camera. The usual arrangement is for the actors to stand in an irregular line from one side of the screen to the other, with the actors at the end coming forward a little and standing more in profile than the others. The purpose of the composition is to allow complex [dialogue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialogue "Dialogue") scenes to be played out without changes in camera position.[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cite_note-8) 

[anamorphic format](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anamorphic_format "Anamorphic format")
    1.The technique of shooting a [widescreen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#widescreen) picture on visual recording media with a non-widescreen native [aspect ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#aspect_ratio).     2.A projection format in which a distorted image is "stretched" by an anamorphic projection lens to recreate the original aspect ratio on the viewing screen. 

[anamorphic widescreen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anamorphic_widescreen "Anamorphic widescreen")


angle of light


[angle of view](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_of_view "Angle of view")


angle plus angle


[angular resolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_resolution "Angular resolution")


[animation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animation "Animation")


[animatronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animatronics "Animatronics")


[answer print](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Answer_print "Answer print")
    The first version of a given [motion picture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#motion_picture) that is printed to film after [color correction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_correction "Color correction") on an [interpositive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#interpositive). It is also the first version of the movie printed to film with the sound properly synced to the picture.[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cite_note-9) 

[aperture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aperture "Aperture")


[apple box](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_box "Apple box")


[Armorer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weapons_master "Weapons master")
    A member of the shooting crew who handles, maintains, and is responsible for real and [prop weapon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prop#Weapons "Prop") safety on set.[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cite_note-10) 

[art department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_department "Art department")


[artificial light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_light "Artificial light")


[ASA speed rating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_speed "Film speed")


[aspect ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aspect_ratio_\(image\) "Aspect ratio \(image\)")


[autofocus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autofocus "Autofocus")


[automated dialogue replacement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automated_dialogue_replacement "Automated dialogue replacement")

Also called **looping** or a **looping session**.
    The process of re-recording dialogue by the original actor after the filming process to improve audio quality or reflect dialogue changes.[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cite_note-11)[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cite_note-12) In India the process is simply known as "dubbing", while in the [UK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom "United Kingdom"), it is also called "post-synchronisation" or "post-sync". The insertion of [voice acting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#voice_acting) performances for [animation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animation "Animation"), such as [computer-generated imagery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer-generated_imagery "Computer-generated imagery") or [animated cartoons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animated_cartoon "Animated cartoon"), is often referred to as ADR, although it generally does not replace existing dialogue. 

[available light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Available_light "Available light")
    See _[ambient light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#ambient_light)_. 

[axial cut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axial_cut "Axial cut")
    A type of [jump cut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#jump_cut), where the camera suddenly moves closer to or further away from its subject along an invisible line drawn straight between the camera and the subject.[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cite_note-13) While a plain jump cut typically involves a temporal discontinuity (an apparent jump in time), an axial cut is a way of maintaining the illusion of [continuity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#continuity).[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cite_note-hitchcock-14) Axial cuts are used rarely in contemporary cinema but were fairly common in the cinema of the 1910s and 1920s.[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cite_note-bordwell-15)

    A film technique of inserting a [shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#shot) into a film to disguise a mistake, or a [cut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cut).[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cite_note-20)[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cite_note-21)[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cite_note-22)[[23]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cite_note-23) For example, if Character A is talking to Character B and the microphone is briefly in shot while Character A is talking, the editor could insert a shot of Character B listening or reacting, to cover up the mistake. ## C

[C-clamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C-Clamp_\(stagecraft\) "C-Clamp \(stagecraft\)")


[call time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Call_time "Call time")
    The time at which the crew is expected to be on set for work. 

[Callier effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Callier_effect "Callier effect")


[cameo appearance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cameo_appearance "Cameo appearance")


[cameo lighting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cameo_lighting "Cameo lighting")
    A type of lighting whereby a [spotlight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#spotlight) accentuates a single [character](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#character) or other subject and sometimes a few props in a [scene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#scene), so that the focus of the scene is on the subject and not its surrounding environment. It is often used to create an "angelic" shot, such as one where a light used to represent God or heaven shines down onto the character. Cameo lighting derives its name from the art form in which a light relief figure is set against a darker background. It is often achieved by using [barn-doored spotlights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#barn_doors). A problem with cameo lighting is that it can lead to color distortion and noise in the darkest areas. [[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cite_note-25) 

[camera angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera_angle "Camera angle")
    The specific location and position from which a [movie camera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#movie_camera) or [video camera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#video_camera) is oriented to take a [shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#shot). A single scene may be shot from several camera angles simultaneously.[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cite_note-Pincus-26)

[camera dolly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera_dolly "Camera dolly")
    A wheeled cart or similar apparatus to which a camera is mounted so that the camera can be moved horizontally in order to capture smooth, natural [motion shots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#motion_shot). Often but not necessarily operated upon a set of rails or a track, a dolly is generally used to produce images which involve the camera moving toward or away from a subject, or [panning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#panning) across a scene from side to side. A [dolly grip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#dolly_grip) may be responsible for manually pushing the dolly back and forth. 

[camera operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera_operator "Camera operator")

Also **cameraman** or **camerawoman**.
    A member of a film [crew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#crew) who operates a film camera or digital video camera.

[chroma keying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chroma_keying "Chroma keying")

Also called **chroma key compositing** , **color keying** , or **color-separation overlay (CSO)**.
    A [post-production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#post-production) [visual effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#visual_effects) technique for [compositing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#compositing) or layering two images or video streams together based on color hues: a specific range of color(s) in the background of the first shot is made transparent, allowing separately recorded footage or a static image to be displayed in the transparent areas instead, giving the appearance that the foreground subject of the first shot is in front of a particular background environment. Chroma keying can be done with any color from the first shot that is uniform and distinct from the colors of the intended foreground subject, but green and blue are commonly used because the subject is often a human and these differ most distinctly in hue from human skin colors.

[clapperboard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clapperboard "Clapperboard")
    A device used in filmmaking and video production to assist in the synchronizing of picture and sound, and to identify and mark the various [scenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#scene) and [takes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#take) as they are filmed and audio-recorded. Use of a clapperboard can make [editing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#film_editing) and arranging film sequences easier during [post-production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#post-production). [colorization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_colorization "Film colorization")

Modern colorization is usually achieved with digital image processing software. 

[compositing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compositing "Compositing")
    The process or technique of combining visual elements from separate sources into a single image, particularly so as to create the illusion that all of those elements are parts of the same [scene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#scene). In modern cinema, compositing is usually achieved through digital image manipulation in [post-production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#post-production).

continuity error
    A mistake in the apparent [continuity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#continuity) of a motion picture or its [script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#script) as it is presented to and understood by the audience; e.g. an object in a [scene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#scene) that is present in one [shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#shot) subsequently being absent or in a different position in the next shot, without any obvious explanation given as to how or why the object might have moved. Continuity errors are often the result of shots or scenes which are ultimately presented consecutively in the finished film being filmed out of sequence or under different shooting conditions during production, such that the natural continuity intended in the scenes is lost and must instead be simulated by the filmmaker.

[cutaway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cutaway_\(filmmaking\) "Cutaway \(filmmaking\)")


[cut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cut_\(transition\) "Cut \(transition\)")
    An abrupt but usually trivial [transition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#transition) from one [shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#shot) or sequence to another, consisting simply of the sudden termination of the first shot and the immediate beginning of the next, without any delay or interspersed effects. The term originally referred to the physical cutting of a film strip and the subsequent splicing together of non-adjacent [frames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#frame), but is now also used to refer to a similar process in computer [editing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#film_editing) software. It is often used interchangeably with the term [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#edit), though edit may also imply any number of transitions or effects.

## D
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: D")] 

[dailies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dailies "Dailies")
    The raw, unedited footage shot during the making of a motion picture, traditionally the first positive prints developed from the negatives filmed on the previous day, in a process that in the era of physical film reels took place overnight. The dailies were typically reviewed by the [director](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#director) and other [cast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cast) and [crew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#crew) members the next day. With the advent of [digital filmmaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#digital_film), dailies became available instantly after each [take](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#take) and the review process was no longer tied to the overnight development of film. 

[daily call sheet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daily_call_sheet "Daily call sheet")
    The schedule crafted by the [assistant director](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assistant_director "Assistant director"), using the [director](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#director)'s shot list. It is issued to the cast and crew of a film production to inform them of when and where they should report for a particular day of filming.[[29]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cite_note-29) The production schedule is usually listed by _call time_ , the time when people are expected to start work on a [film set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#film_set). 

[daily editor log](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daily_editor_log "Daily editor log")


[daily production report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daily_production_report "Daily production report")

[day for night](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Day_for_night "Day for night")
    A set of cinematic techniques used to simulate a nighttime [scene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#scene) while filming in daylight, often employed when it is too difficult or expensive to actually shoot during nighttime. When shooting day for night, the otherwise bright daytime footage is typically underexposed in-camera or artificially darkened during [post-production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#post-production), often with a blue tint added; additional effects may also be used to give the impression of genuine darkness. 

[Day Out of Days](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Day_out_of_days_\(filmmaking\) "Day out of days \(filmmaking\)")
    A chart used by filmmakers to tally the number of paid days for each cast member.

[digital audio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_audio "Digital audio")

[digital cinema](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_cinema "Digital cinema")


[digital cinematography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_cinematography "Digital cinematography")
    The process of recording a [motion picture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#motion_picture) using digital image sensors rather than physical [film stock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#film_stock). [Dutch angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_angle "Dutch angle")

Also called **Dutch tilt** , **canted angle** , or **oblique angle**.
    A type of camera [shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#shot) where the camera is set at an angle on its roll axis so that the shot is composed with vertical lines at an angle to the side of the frame, or, equivalently, so that the horizon line of the shot is not parallel with the bottom of the camera frame. This produces a viewpoint akin to tilting one's head to the side.[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cite_note-auto-31) In [cinematography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cinematography), the Dutch angle is one of many [cinematic techniques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinematic_techniques "Cinematic techniques") used to portray psychological uneasiness or tension in the subject being filmed.

[establishing shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Establishing_shot "Establishing shot")
    Any [shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#shot) which sets up or establishes the context for a [scene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#scene) by showing the relationship between its important figures and objects.[[28]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cite_note-classesyale-28) It is often a [long](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#long_shot) or [extreme long shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#extreme_long_shot) at the beginning of a scene indicating where, and sometimes when, the remainder of the scene takes place.[[32]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cite_note-calgary-32)[[33]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cite_note-mediacollege-33)[[34]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cite_note-Purdue-34)[[35]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cite_note-guillotine-35)

[external rhythm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/External_rhythm "External rhythm")

Also called **cutting rhythm**.
    The sense of movement or "flow" established by the duration of the [shots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#shot) (and consequently the frequency of [cuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cut)) that comprise a [scene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#scene) or sequence. Lengthening or shortening the duration of the shots establishes an often subconscious rhythmic pattern that may complement or contrast with the [internal rhythm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#internal_rhythm) and content of a scene or sequence. 

[extra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extra_\(acting\) "Extra \(acting\)")

Also called a **background actor**.
    An [actor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#actor) or performer in a film who appears in a non-speaking or non-singing capacity, usually briefly and in the background, without any particular characterization or direct plot relevance, such that viewers are not intended to identify with or consciously focus on the character at all. Extras are often employed in large numbers in war films or epic films, or in scenes depicting crowded city streets, with the sole purpose of creating a sense of scale by populating the scene with activity. They generally have the most minimal roles of any persons considered [cast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cast) members, and though they are usually required to be paid, they are sometimes not even trained actors.

## F

[feature film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feature_film "Feature film")

Also **feature-length film**.
    A narrative film with a [running time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#running_time) long enough for it to be considered the principal or feature presentation in a commercial entertainment program. The term originally referred to the main, full-length film in early cinema programs that also included one or more [short films](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#short_film), [newsreels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#newsreel), or advertisements presented before the main event. In modern usage the term more commonly indicates simply that a film is of a substantial length or running time, as distinguished from short films, though what is considered "[feature length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#feature_length)" can vary and has changed over time. 

[feature length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feature_film "Feature film")
    The minimum [running time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#running_time) necessary to be considered a full-length or [feature film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#feature_film), as opposed to a [short film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#short_film). Most films between 75 and 210 minutes in duration are said to be feature-length, but the precise definition can vary. 

[field of view](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_of_view "Field of view")


[fill light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fill_light "Fill light")
    One of three sources of light in a traditional [three-point lighting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#three-point_lighting) set-up, generally placed to one side of the subject being filmed or photographed, opposite the [back light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#back_light) and approximately perpendicular to the [key light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#key_light). The fill light is often used to reduce the [contrast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contrast_\(vision\) "Contrast \(vision\)") of a scene in order to match on the recording media the level of detail typically seen by the human eye in real-world lighting conditions. 

[film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film "Film")


[film budgeting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_budgeting "Film budgeting")


[film crew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_crew "Film crew")
    The collective term for a group of people hired by a [film production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#film_production) company for the purpose of [producing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#filmmaking) a [film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#film) or [motion picture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#motion_picture). The crew is distinguished from the [cast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cast), which is generally understood to consist solely of the [actors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#actor) who appear in front of the camera or [provide voices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#voice_acting) for characters in the film, as well as from the [producers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#producer), who own at least a portion of the production company or the film's intellectual property rights. 

[film criticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_criticism "Film criticism")


[film finance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_finance "Film finance")


[film gate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_gate "Film gate")
    The rectangular opening in the front of a motion picture camera through which the [film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#film_stock) is exposed to light. It can be seen by removing the lens and rotating the shutter out of the way. A pressure plate behind the film holds the film on a uniform [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#film_plane) at a calibrated distance from the gate. 

[film genre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_genre "Film genre")


[film inventory report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_inventory_report "Film inventory report")


[film modification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_modification "Film modification")


[film plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_plane "Film plane")
    The surface inside a camera upon which the lens creates a focused image. Each lens used with the camera must be calibrated carefully to ensure that the image is focused on the exact spot where the individual [frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#frame) of [film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#film_stock) or digital sensor is positioned during exposure. 

[film production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_production "Film production")


[film recorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_recorder "Film recorder")


[film release](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_release "Film release")


[film scanner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_scanner "Film scanner")


[film score](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_score "Film score")
    A piece of original music written specifically to accompany a film. A score is typically divided into a number of orchestral, instrumental, or choral pieces called cues, which are timed to begin and end at specific points during the film in order to enhance the dramatic narrative and the emotional impact of particular scenes. 

[film speed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_speed "Film speed")


[film stock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_stock "Film stock")
    An analog recording medium for [motion pictures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#motion_picture) that is recorded on by a movie camera, developed, edited, and then projected on to a screen by a [projector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#projector). Film stock consists of a long strip of transparent plastic [film base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_base "Film base") coated on one side with a gelatin emulsion containing microscopic light-sensitive [silver halide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver_halide "Silver halide") crystals. When briefly exposed to the light admitted by a camera lens, a subtle chemical change occurs that is proportional to the amount of light absorbed by each crystal, creating an invisible latent image in the emulsion which can then be developed through further chemical treatment into a visible photograph. Film stock made of nitrate, acetate, or polyester bases is the traditional medium for capturing the numerous [frames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#frame) of a motion picture, widely used until the emergence of [digital film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#digital_film) in the late 20th century. 

[film theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_theory "Film theory")

## G
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: G")] 

[gobo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gobo_\(lighting\) "Gobo \(lighting\)")
    An object placed inside or in front of a light source to control the shape of the emitted light and its shadow. 

[go motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Go_motion "Go motion")
    A type of [stop-motion animation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#stop-motion_animation) which attempts to simulate [motion blur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#motion_blur) in each [frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#frame) involving motion. Ordinary stop-motion animation can result in a disorienting "staccato" effect because the animated object has a perfectly sharp appearance in every frame (since each frame was actually shot when the object was perfectly still); by contrast, objects that are actually moving will appear slightly blurry, because they moved while the shutter of the camera was open. Go motion attempts to recreate this blur effect so that the animation appears more natural, as if the animated object was actually moving in each frame.

## K
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms&action=edit&section=12 "Edit section: K")] 

[key grip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Key_grip "Key grip")
    A senior [crew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#crew) member who is responsible for supervising the [grip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#grip) crew, typically in order to perform tasks such as assessing what equipment is necessary at each shooting location, coordinating the transportation and set-up of this equipment, and arranging for the general movement and positioning of the camera. The key grip collaborates closely with the [director of photography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#director_of_photography) and relies on the [best boy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#best_boy) as their chief assistant. 

[key light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Key_light "Key light")
    One of three light sources in a traditional [three-point lighting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#three-point_lighting) set-up, generally positioned directly in front of the subject being filmed or photographed and supplemented by the [back light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#back_light) and [fill light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#fill_light). The key light is usually the first and most important light to be considered when staging a scene. Its purpose is to highlight the form and dimension of the subject; omitting the key light can result in a [silhouette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silhouette "Silhouette") effect. [![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d8/3_point_lighting.svg/250px-3_point_lighting.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:3_point_lighting.svg)A diagram of a standard **[three-point lighting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#three-point_lighting)** set-up, consisting of a **[key light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#key_light)** , **[back light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#back_light)** , and **[fill light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#fill_light)** 

[Kuleshov effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuleshov_effect "Kuleshov effect")
    The mental phenomenon by which viewers of a film interpret the juxtaposition of and interaction between sequential [shots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#shot) in a way that differs from their interpretation of either of the individual shots alone. This effect can be demonstrated by comparing viewers' reactions to two different sequences, each featuring an identical shot preceded or followed by different shots that suggest or evoke distinct associations; viewers tend to ascribe different emotions or meanings to the identical shots based on the mental associations implied by the adjacent shots. The effect illustrates how the interpretation of any individual shot in a motion picture depends largely on the shots immediately adjacent to it – no single shot exists in isolation.

[L cut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L_cut "L cut")
    A type of [split edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#split_edit) in which the picture [cuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cut) before the audio, such that the audio of the preceding [shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#shot) or [scene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#scene) overlaps the picture from the following scene; i.e. the audio of the previous scene (often dialogue or narration) continues to play over the beginning of the next scene before cutting or fading. 

[leading actor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leading_actor "Leading actor")


[leitmotif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leitmotif "Leitmotif")


[lens flare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lens_flare "Lens flare")


[letterboxing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letterboxing_\(filming\) "Letterboxing \(filming\)")
    The process of transferring film shot in a [widescreen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#widescreen) [aspect ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#aspect_ratio) to standard-width video formats while preserving the film's original aspect ratio. Doing so necessarily results in each [frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#frame) of the video signal being lined with horizontal mattes, usually empty black strips, above and below it. Letterboxing is common in feature films formatted for [home video](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#home_video) playback on standard-width television screens. 

[limited release](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limited_theatrical_release "Limited theatrical release")
    A type of film distribution in which a film is shown in just a small fraction of the movie theaters available in a region or country, typically only in major metropolitan markets and often at small-scale independently owned theaters; in the U.S. and Canada, a limited release is defined as a film released in less than 600 theaters nationwide. Contrast _[wide release](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#wide_release)_. 

lockup
    An area being cordoned off and controlled by crew members to prevent either unwanted sound or pedestrians from ruining the [take](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#take). 

[long shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_shot "Long shot")


[long take](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_take "Long take")
    A [shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#shot) or [take](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#take) with a duration much longer than the conventional [editing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#film_editing) pace used in the rest of the film or of films in general. Significant camera movement and elaborate [blocking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#blocking) techniques are often though not always elements in long takes. 

[low-angle shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low-angle_shot "Low-angle shot")


[low-key lighting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low-key_lighting "Low-key lighting")

## M
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms&action=edit&section=14 "Edit section: M")] 

[Martini Shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martini_Shot "Martini Shot")

Also called the **Window Shot**.
    A term used in [Hollywood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinema_of_the_United_States "Cinema of the United States") for the final [shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#shot) set-up of the day. The Martini Shot was so named because "the next shot is out of a [glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shot_glass "Shot glass")", referring to a post-wrap drink.[[40]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cite_note-40) 

[master shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_shot "Master shot")


[match cut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Match_cut "Match cut")
    A type of [cut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cut) from one [shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#shot) to another where the composition of each shot is matched to the other by the action or subject matter depicted; e.g. in a scene depicting a duel, a [long shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#long_shot) showing both of the duellists might cut to a [close-up](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#close-up) shot of one of the duellists in the midst of the action. Match cuts are precisely timed and coordinated so as to produce a seamless transition that is consistent with the logic of the action. 

[match moving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Match_moving "Match moving")


[medium close-up](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Close-up#Close-up_types "Close-up")


[medium shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medium_shot "Medium shot")


[Mexican filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_filter "Mexican filter")
    Yellow color filter sometimes applied in films to depict Mexican locations. 

[MIDI timecode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIDI_timecode "MIDI timecode")


[mise-en-scène](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mise-en-sc%C3%A8ne "Mise-en-scène")


[money shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Money_shot "Money shot")


[montage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montage_\(filmmaking\) "Montage \(filmmaking\)")


[Morris the Explainer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morris_the_Explainer "Morris the Explainer")
    A term referring to a fictional character (by whatever name) whose job it is to explain the plot or parts of a plot to other characters and the audience. 

[mood lighting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mood_lighting "Mood lighting")
    The deliberate use of certain [lighting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#lighting) characteristics in a scene or even an entire film in order to provoke a particular state of mind or feeling in the viewer. 

[MOS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MOS_\(film\) "MOS \(film\)")

[panning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panning_\(camera\) "Panning \(camera\)")
    Pivoting or swivelling a camera horizontally about a fixed vertical axis, usually somewhat slowly, similar to the motion of a person turning their head from left to right. In the resulting image, new material appears on one side of the screen as it exits from the other; perspective lines may or may not be conspicuous enough to reveal to the viewer that the entire image is being seen from a stationary vantage point (as opposed to the camera as a whole moving, as if mounted on a camera dolly).

## R
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms&action=edit&section=18 "Edit section: R")] 

[racking focus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racking_focus "Racking focus")


[reaction shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reaction_shot "Reaction shot")
    A shot which cuts away from the main scene in order to show the reaction of a character to it. It is a basic unit of [film grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#film_grammar). 

[read-through](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Read-through "Read-through")

Also **table-read** or **table work**.
    An organized live reading of the [screenplay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#screenplay) or script of a film, television, radio, or theatre production, usually in an early stage of production and with the [actors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#actor) cast in each speaking role present around a table and reading their respective parts. Non-speaking parts and those that have not yet been cast may be read by stand-ins or members of the production team. 

[reel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reel#Motion_picture_terminology "Reel")


[reflector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflector_\(photography\) "Reflector \(photography\)")


[Rembrandt lighting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rembrandt_lighting "Rembrandt lighting")
    A lighting technique used in studio portrait photography and cinematography which is characterized by the presence of an illuminated triangle beneath the eye of the subject on the less illuminated side of the face. This effect is generally achieved with the use of a single light source positioned to one side of the subject and a [reflector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#reflector) on the opposite side, and is popular because it is capable of producing images which appear both natural and compelling with a minimum of equipment. 

[replay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instant_replay "Instant replay")


[reveal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reveal_\(narrative\) "Reveal \(narrative\)")
    A plot device in narrative structure in which a previously unseen key character or element of the plot is exposed to the reader or audience for the first time. Major reveals often occur at critical moments in the development of the plot, such as the [climax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#climax). 

[reverse angle shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reverse_angle_shot "Reverse angle shot")


[roadshow theatrical release](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roadshow_theatrical_release "Roadshow theatrical release")


roll
    1.A spool or core-load of [film stock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#film_stock).     2.A command to a film crew to start recording a scene with cameras and sound recorders, and/or to the cast to proceed with the acting out of a scene from a certain point.     3.The rotation of a camera around the lens axis. Contrast _[pitch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#pitch)_ and _[yaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#yaw)_. 

[rotoscoping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotoscoping "Rotoscoping")
    An animation technique to capture realistic motion by tracing live action frame by frame.[[44]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cite_note-44) 

[rough cut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rough_cut "Rough cut")
    The second of three stages of offline film [editing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#editing), in which shots and sequences are laid out in approximate relationship and chronology, without detailed attention to the individual [cutting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cut) points.

## S
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms&action=edit&section=19 "Edit section: S")] 

[scene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scene_\(performing_arts\) "Scene \(performing arts\)")


[screen direction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Screen_direction "Screen direction")
    The direction that actors or objects appear to be moving on the screen from the point of view of the camera or the audience. A fundamental rule of [film grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#film_grammar) and [film editing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#film_editing) is that movement from one edited shot to another must maintain consistency of screen direction in order to avoid audience confusion. 

[screenplay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Screenplay "Screenplay")

Also **script**.
    A written work by [screenwriters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#screenwriter) for a film, television show, or video game. Like theatrical playbooks, screenplays are a form of narration in which the movements, actions, expressions, and dialogue of the characters are explicitly described in a specific format; visual or cinematographic cues may also be written, as well as descriptions of [scenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#scene) and scene changes. Screenplays can be original works or [adaptations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#adaptation) from existing pieces of writing. 

[screenwriter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Screenwriter "Screenwriter")


[screenwriting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Screenwriting "Screenwriting")


[script breakdown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Script_breakdown "Script breakdown")


[script supervisor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Script_supervisor "Script supervisor")


[scriptment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scriptment "Scriptment")


[sequence shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_take#Sequence_shot "Long take")


[shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shot_\(filmmaking\) "Shot \(filmmaking\)")
    A series of [frames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#frame) that runs for an uninterrupted period of time. In production, the term refers more specifically to the period between the moment the camera starts rolling and the moment it stops; in [film editing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#film_editing), it refers to the continuous [footage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#footage) or sequence between two consecutive edits or [cuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cut). Shots are essential aspects of all screen productions, including both film and television, where angles, transitions, and cuts are used by the filmmaker to further express emotions, ideas, and movement. 

[shot/reverse shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shot/reverse_shot "Shot/reverse shot")
    A film technique in which one character is shown looking at another character, and then the other character is shown looking back at the first character in a separate [shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#shot). Since the characters are shown facing in opposite directions, the audience is led to assume that they are looking at each other. 

[shooting in the round](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shooting_in_the_round "Shooting in the round")
    A style of cinematography in which the [180-degree rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#180-degree_rule) is broken and the [actors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#actor) are filmed from all sides. 

[shooting schedule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shooting_schedule "Shooting schedule")


[shooting script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shooting_script "Shooting script")


[single-camera setup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-camera_setup "Single-camera setup")


[slow cutting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slow_cutting "Slow cutting")
    A film editing technique which uses [shots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#shot) of long duration, i.e. with [cuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cut) occurring at long intervals. Most shots longer than about 15 seconds seem slow to modern-day Western audiences accustomed to mainstream films, where slow cuts are uncommon. 

[slow motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slow_motion "Slow motion")


[smash cut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smash_cut "Smash cut")
    A technique of [film editing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#film_editing) in which one [scene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#scene) abruptly [cuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cut) to another for aesthetic, narrative, or emotional effect. To this end, the smash cut usually occurs at a crucial moment in a scene where the audience would not expect a cut; to heighten the impact, a disparity in the type of scene on either side of the cut is often emphasized, e.g. cutting from a tense or fast-paced scene to a pleasant or tranquil one. 

[SMPTE timecode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMPTE_timecode "SMPTE timecode")
    A set of cooperating standards for labeling individual [frames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#frame) of video or film with a [timecode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#timecode), defined by the [Society of Motion Picture and Television Engineers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#society_of_motion_picture_and_television_engineers). 

[soft light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soft_light "Soft light")


[sound design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_design#Film "Sound design")


[sound designer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_design#Film "Sound design")


[sound editor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_editor_\(filmmaking\) "Sound editor \(filmmaking\)")


[sound effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_effect "Sound effect")

Often abbreviated in the plural as **SFX**. [sound report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_report "Sound report")


[spec script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spec_script "Spec script")

Also **speculative screenplay**.
    A non-commissioned and unsolicited [screenplay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#screenplay) or film [treatment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#treatment), i.e. one that is written of a [screenwriter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#screenwriter)'s own accord, usually with the intention of having the script optioned and eventually purchased by a [producer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#producer), production company, or film studio. 

[split edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Split_edit "Split edit")


[split screen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Split_screen_\(video_production\) "Split screen \(video production\)")


[special effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_effect "Special effect")


[special effects supervisor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_effects_supervisor "Special effects supervisor")


[stage lighting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stage_lighting "Stage lighting")


[stalker vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stalker_vision "Stalker vision")

Also called **monster vision**. [stand-in](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stand-in "Stand-in")
    A person who substitutes for an [actor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#actor) during an early stage of film or television production, prior to actual filming, often for technical purposes such as [lighting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#lighting) and camera set-up but also for [script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#screenplay) and [storyboarding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#storyboard) purposes such as a [table-read](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#table-read), especially when the actors intended to portray certain characters have not yet been cast. 

[Steadicam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steadicam "Steadicam")
    A popular brand of [camera-stabilizing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#camera_stabilizer) mounts for film cameras invented by [Garrett Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garrett_Brown "Garrett Brown") and introduced in 1975 by [Cinema Products Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinema_Products_Corporation "Cinema Products Corporation"). These mounts mechanically isolate the operator's movement, allowing for a smooth shot even when the camera moves over an irregular surface. 

[step outline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Step_outline "Step outline")


[stop motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stop_motion "Stop motion")


[storyboard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Storyboard "Storyboard")
    A graphical arrangement of images or illustrations depicting the scenes and characters of a motion picture, animation, or interactive media production and displayed in sequence for the purpose of allowing writers, directors, or artists to pre-visualize and easily modify the chronology and structure of the narrative and/or the compositions of and transitions between particular shots. The storyboarding process is one of the earliest steps in [pre-production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#pre-production).

[stunt performer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stunt_performer "Stunt performer")

## T
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms&action=edit&section=20 "Edit section: T")] 

[take](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Take "Take")
    A single continuous recording of a performance, often denoted with a number and used to track the stages of filming, especially the filming of a particular [scene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#scene). Multiple takes may be filmed for the same scene or performance so as to provide editors with more than one option from which to choose when [editing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#film_editing). 

[test screening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Test_screening "Test screening")
    A preview showing of a film or television show prior to a wider general release in order to gauge audience reaction. 

[tilt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tilt_\(camera\) "Tilt \(camera\)")
    A [cinematographic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cinematography) technique in which the camera remains in a fixed position but pivots up or down in a vertical [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_\(mathematics\) "Plane \(mathematics\)"), i.e. upon its own x-axis.[[45]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cite_note-45) Tilting the camera results in a motion similar to someone raising or lowering their head to look up or down. It is distinguished from [panning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#panning), in which the camera is pivoted horizontally left or right. Pan and tilt can be used simultaneously.[[46]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cite_note-46) In some situations the lens itself may be tilted with respect to the fixed camera body in order to generate greater [depth of focus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#depth_of_focus).[[47]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cite_note-47)[[48]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cite_note-48) [![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/e5/Tilt1.gif?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail_unscaled)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Tilt1.gif)**[Tilting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#tilt)** a camera 

[timecode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timecode "Timecode")

Also **time code**.
    A sequence of numeric codes generated at regular intervals by a timing [synchronization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synchronization "Synchronization") system and commonly used in [video production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#video_production) and other applications which require temporal coordination or logging of recordings or actions. 

[time-lapse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time-lapse "Time-lapse")
    A technique in which the frequency at which film [frames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#frame) are captured is much lower than the frequency at which they are played back when viewing the sequence. When played at a normal playback speed, time appears to be moving faster or "lapsing". For example, if images of a scene are captured at a rate of 1 [frame per second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#frames_per_second) but then played back at a standard 30 frames per second, the resulting sequence appears to be occurring 30 times faster than it did in reality. Time-lapse photography is the opposite of [high-speed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#high-speed_photography) or slow-motion photography, in which film is captured at a much higher rate than at which it is played back, appearing to slow down an otherwise fast action. A **[time-lapse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#time-lapse)** film of plant seeds germinating 

[title sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Title_sequence "Title sequence")


[tracking shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tracking_shot "Tracking shot")
    Any [shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#shot) in which the camera follows backward, forward, or moves alongside the subject being filmed. The camera may be mounted on a [dolly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#camera_dolly) designed to move along a dedicated track, or it may be moved manually via a handheld [steadycam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#steadycam) or [gimbal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#gimbal). Tracking shots are often long, continuous sequences lasting multiple seconds. 

[trunk shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trunk_shot "Trunk shot")


[two shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_shot "Two shot")


[typecasting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typecasting_\(acting\) "Typecasting \(acting\)")

## V
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms&action=edit&section=21 "Edit section: V")] 

[video production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_production "Video production")


[videography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videography "Videography")
    The process of capturing [moving images](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#motion_picture) on electronic media or [streaming media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#streaming_media), including in the broadest sense methods of [video production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#video_production) and [post-production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#post-production). Once considered the video equivalent of [cinematography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#cinematography), the emergence of digital video recording technologies has blurred the distinction between the two, such that in modern usage any type of video recording (e.g. television news broadcasting) may be referred to as videography, while cinematography is usually reserved for large-scale commercial motion picture production. 

[visual effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_effects "Visual effects")


[visual effects supervisor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_effects_supervisor "Visual effects supervisor")


[voice acting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voice_acting "Voice acting")
    The art of performing [voice-overs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#voice-over) to present a character or to provide narration to an audience. 

[voice actor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voice_acting "Voice acting")

Also **voice artist** , **voice-over artist** , and **voice talent**.
    An [actor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#actor) who performs using only their voice, i.e. through [voice-overs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#voice-over). Voice acting is used especially in radio productions and [animated films](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#animated_film), where the actual providers of the voices of speaking characters are never seen, but is also commonly used for narration in [live-action films](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_motion_picture_terms#live-action_film). Many voice actors are not exclusively voice actors but also act in standard non-voice roles in other productions. 

[voice artist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voice_artist "Voice artist")


[voice-over](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voice-over "Voice-over")

Also called **off-camera commentary** or **off-stage commentary**.
