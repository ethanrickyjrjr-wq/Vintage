From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Lines radiating from bright light sources in photographs
[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/34/NGC6397.jpg/250px-NGC6397.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:NGC6397.jpg)Diffraction spikes from various stars seen on an image taken by the [Hubble Space Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubble_Space_Telescope "Hubble Space Telescope")
**Diffraction spikes** are lines radiating from bright light sources, causing what is known as the **starburst effect**[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffraction_spike#cite_note-1) or **sunstars**[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffraction_spike#cite_note-2) in photographs and in vision. They are [artifacts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artifact_\(error\) "Artifact \(error\)") caused by light [diffracting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffraction "Diffraction") around the support vanes of the secondary mirror in [reflecting telescopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflecting_telescope "Reflecting telescope"), or edges of non-circular camera [apertures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aperture "Aperture"), and around eyelashes and eyelids in the eye.
While similar in appearance, this is a different effect to "vertical smear" or "blooming" that appears when bright light sources are captured by a [charge-coupled device (CCD)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge-coupled_device "Charge-coupled device") image sensor.
## Causes
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Diffraction_spike&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Causes")]
### Support vanes
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Diffraction_spike&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Support vanes")]
[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/df/Comparison_strut_diffraction_spikes.svg/960px-Comparison_strut_diffraction_spikes.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Comparison_strut_diffraction_spikes.svg)
Comparison of diffraction spikes for various strut arrangements of a reflecting telescope. The inner circle represents the secondary mirror.
[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e0/Newtonianscope-inside.JPG/250px-Newtonianscope-inside.JPG?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Newtonianscope-inside.JPG)
The optics of a [Newtonian reflector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newtonian_reflector "Newtonian reflector") telescope with four [spider vanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spider_vane "Spider vane") supporting the secondary mirror.
In the vast majority of [reflecting telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflecting_telescope "Reflecting telescope") designs, the secondary mirror has to be positioned at the central axis of the telescope and so has to be held by struts within the telescope tube. No matter how fine these support rods are, they [diffract](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffraction "Diffraction") the incoming light from a subject star. This appears as diffraction spikes, which are the [Fourier transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_transform "Fourier transform") of the support struts. The spikes represent a loss of light that could have been used to image the star.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffraction_spike#cite_note-3)[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffraction_spike#cite_note-4)
Although diffraction spikes can obscure parts of a photograph and are undesired in professional contexts, some [amateur astronomers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amateur_astronomy "Amateur astronomy") like the visual effect they give to bright stars–the "[Star of Bethlehem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_of_Bethlehem "Star of Bethlehem")" appearance–and even modify their refractors to exhibit the same effect,[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffraction_spike#cite_note-5) or to assist with focusing when using a [CCD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge-coupled_device "Charge-coupled device").[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffraction_spike#cite_note-6)
A small number of reflecting telescopes designs avoid diffraction spikes by placing the secondary mirror off-axis. Early off-axis designs such as the [Herschelian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflecting_telescope#Herschelian "Reflecting telescope") and the [Schiefspiegler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflecting_telescope#Schiefspiegler "Reflecting telescope") telescopes have serious limitations such as [astigmatism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astigmatism_\(optical_systems\) "Astigmatism \(optical systems\)") and long focal ratios, which make them useless for research. The brachymedial design by [Ludwig Schupmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Schupmann "Ludwig Schupmann"), which uses a combination of mirrors and lenses, is able to correct [chromatic aberration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatic_aberration "Chromatic aberration") perfectly over a small area and designs based on the Schupmann brachymedial are currently used for research of [double stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_star "Double star").
There are also a small number of off-axis unobstructed all-reflecting [anastigmats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anastigmat "Anastigmat") which give optically perfect images.
[Refracting telescopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refracting_telescope "Refracting telescope") and their photographic images do not have the same problem as their lenses are not supported with spider vanes.
### Segmented mirrors
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Diffraction_spike&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Segmented mirrors")]
[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a4/FOFC8ZPX0AIB-Ho.png/500px-FOFC8ZPX0AIB-Ho.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:FOFC8ZPX0AIB-Ho.png)

### Non-circular aperture
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Diffraction_spike&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Non-circular aperture")]
[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/47/Comparison_aperture_diffraction_spikes.svg/500px-Comparison_aperture_diffraction_spikes.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Comparison_aperture_diffraction_spikes.svg)
Comparison of diffraction spikes for apertures of different shapes and blade count
[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d7/Apertures.jpg/250px-Apertures.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Apertures.jpg)
Camera aperture blades
[Iris diaphragms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diaphragm_\(optics\)#Iris_diaphragms_versus_other_types "Diaphragm \(optics\)") with moving blades are used in most modern camera lenses to restrict the light received by the film or sensor. While manufacturers attempt to make the [aperture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aperture "Aperture") circular for a pleasing [bokeh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bokeh "Bokeh"), when stopped down to high [f-numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F-number "F-number") (small apertures), its shape tends towards a polygon with the same number of sides as blades. Diffraction spreads out light waves passing through the aperture perpendicular to the roughly-straight edge, each edge yielding two spikes 180° apart.[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffraction_spike#cite_note-kingslake-8) As the blades are uniformly distributed around the circle, on a diaphragm with an even number of blades, the diffraction spikes from blades on opposite sides overlap. Consequently, a diaphragm with _n_ blades yields _n_ spikes if _n_ is even, and 2 _n_ spikes if _n_ is odd.[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffraction_spike#cite_note-9)

### Dirty optics
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Diffraction_spike&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Dirty optics")]
[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a3/57-night_view_in_Brea.jpg/250px-57-night_view_in_Brea.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:57-night_view_in_Brea.jpg)Streaks due to a dirty lens
An improperly cleaned lens or cover glass, or one with a fingerprint may have parallel lines which diffract light similarly to support vanes.[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffraction_spike#cite_note-10)
They can be distinguished from [spikes due to non-circular aperture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffraction_spike#Diffraction_spikes_due_to_non-circular_aperture) as they form a prominent smear in a single direction, and from [CCD bloom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge-coupled_device#Blooming "Charge-coupled device") by their oblique angle.
### Obstructions
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Diffraction_spike&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Obstructions")]
  * [![Near the equinoxes in Chicago sunrise and sunset photography of east–west urban street canyons often features diffraction spikes. Similar Manhattanhenge happenings occur throughout the year around the world.](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3c/20240920_53rd_Street_Hyde_Park_Chicago_near_equinox_stacked_rotated_and_cropped.jpg/120px-20240920_53rd_Street_Hyde_Park_Chicago_near_equinox_stacked_rotated_and_cropped.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:20240920_53rd_Street_Hyde_Park_Chicago_near_equinox_stacked_rotated_and_cropped.jpg "Near the equinoxes in Chicago sunrise and sunset photography of east–west urban street canyons often features diffraction spikes. Near the [equinoxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equinox "Equinox") in Chicago sunrise and sunset photography of east–west urban [street canyons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Street_canyon "Street canyon") often features diffraction spikes. Similar [Manhattanhenge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattanhenge "Manhattanhenge") happenings occur throughout the year around the world.

## In vision
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Diffraction_spike&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: In vision")]
In normal vision, diffraction through eyelashes–and due to the edges of the eyelids if one is squinting–produce many diffraction spikes. If it is windy, then the motion of the eyelashes cause spikes that move around and scintillate. After a blink, the eyelashes may come back in a different position and cause the diffraction spikes to jump around. This is classified as an [entoptic phenomenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entoptic_phenomenon "Entoptic phenomenon").
Diffraction spike in normal human vision can also be caused by some fibers in the eye lens sometimes called [suture lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lens_\(vertebrate_anatomy\)#Development "Lens \(vertebrate anatomy\)").[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffraction_spike#cite_note-11)
## Other uses
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Diffraction_spike&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Other uses")]
### Bahtinov mask
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Diffraction_spike&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Bahtinov mask")]
Main article: [Bahtinov mask](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahtinov_mask "Bahtinov mask")
[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/36/Bahtinov_mask_principle.svg/250px-Bahtinov_mask_principle.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Bahtinov_mask_principle.svg)Use of diffraction spikes to focus a telescope with a [Bahtinov mask](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahtinov_mask "Bahtinov mask")
In amateur [astrophotography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrophotography "Astrophotography"), a Bahtinov mask can be used to focus small astronomical telescopes accurately. Light from a bright point such as an isolated bright star reaching different quadrants of the [primary mirror](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_mirror "Primary mirror") or lens is first passed through grilles at three different orientations. Half of the mask generates a narrow "X" shape from four diffraction spikes (blue and green in the illustration); the other half generates a straight line from two spikes (red). Changing the focus causes the shapes to move with respect to each other. When the line passes exactly through the middle of the "X", the telescope is in focus and the mask can be removed.
### Star filter
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Diffraction_spike&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: Star filter")]
A [cross screen filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photographic_filter#Cross_screen "Photographic filter"), also known as a star filter, creates a star pattern using a very fine [diffraction grating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffraction_grating "Diffraction grating") embedded in the filter, or sometimes by the use of prisms in the filter. The number of stars varies by the construction of the filter, as does the number of points each star has.
