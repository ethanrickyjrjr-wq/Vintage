From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Mapping from a Euclidean space to itself
This article is about reflection in geometry. For reflexivity of [binary relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_relation "Binary relation"), see [Reflexive relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflexive_relation "Reflexive relation").
[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9a/SimmetriainvOK.svg/250px-SimmetriainvOK.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:SimmetriainvOK.svg)A reflection of a [chiral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chirality "Chirality") shape through an axis (a diagonal red line here)
In [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics "Mathematics"), a **reflection** (also spelled **reflexion**)[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflection_\(mathematics\)#cite_note-1) is a [mapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Function_\(mathematics\) "Function \(mathematics\)") from a [Euclidean space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_space "Euclidean space") to itself that is an [isometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isometry "Isometry") with a [hyperplane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperplane "Hyperplane") as the set of [fixed points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed_point_\(mathematics\) "Fixed point \(mathematics\)"); this set is called the [axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axis_of_symmetry "Axis of symmetry") (in dimension 2) or [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_\(mathematics\) "Plane \(mathematics\)") (in dimension 3) of reflection. The image of a figure by a reflection is its [mirror image](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirror_image "Mirror image") in the axis or plane of reflection. For example, the reflection of point **A** is **A'** such that the red line is the perpendicular [bisector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bisection "Bisection") of the segment  A A ′ ¯ {\displaystyle {\overline {AA'}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\overline {AA'}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c8ca046d998c6428b1d87368eb64d436a0dc7fe0).[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflection_\(mathematics\)#cite_note-2)
For example, the mirror image of the small Latin letter **p** for a reflection with respect to a [vertical axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertical_axis "Vertical axis") (a _vertical reflection_) would look like **q**. Its image by reflection in a [horizontal axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizontal_axis "Horizontal axis") (a _horizontal reflection_) would look like **b**. A reflection is an [involution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Involution_\(mathematics\) "Involution \(mathematics\)"): when applied twice in succession, every point returns to its original location, and every geometrical object is restored to its original state.
The term _reflection_ is sometimes used for a larger class of mappings from a Euclidean space to itself, namely the non-identity isometries that are involutions. The set of fixed points (the "mirror") of such an isometry is an [affine subspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_subspace "Affine subspace"), but is possibly smaller than a hyperplane.
For instance, a [reflection through a point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_reflection "Point reflection") is an involutive isometry with just one fixed point; the image of the letter **p** under it would look like a **d**. This operation is also known as a [central inversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_reflection "Point reflection") ([Coxeter 1969](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflection_\(mathematics\)#CITEREFCoxeter1969), §7.2), and exhibits Euclidean space as a [symmetric space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetric_space "Symmetric space").
In a [Euclidean vector space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_vector_space "Euclidean vector space"), the reflection in the point situated at the origin is the same as vector negation. Other examples include reflections in a line in three-dimensional space. Typically, however, unqualified use of the term "reflection" means reflection in a [hyperplane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperplane "Hyperplane"). Some mathematicians use "**flip** " as a synonym for "reflection".[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflection_\(mathematics\)#cite_note-3)[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflection_\(mathematics\)#cite_note-4)[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflection_\(mathematics\)#cite_note-5)
## Construction
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Reflection_\(mathematics\)&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Construction")]
[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7c/Perpendicular-construction.svg/250px-Perpendicular-construction.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Perpendicular-construction.svg)Point Q is the reflection of point P through the line AB.
In a plane (or, respectively, 3-dimensional) geometry, to find the reflection of a point drop a [perpendicular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perpendicular "Perpendicular") from the point to the line (plane) used for reflection, and extend it the same distance on the other side. To find the reflection of a figure, reflect each point in the figure.
To reflect point P through the line AB using [compass and straightedge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass_and_straightedge "Compass and straightedge"), proceed as follows (see figure):
  * Step 1 (red): construct a [circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle "Circle") with center at P and some fixed radius _r_ to create points A′ and B′ on the line AB, which will be [equidistant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equidistant "Equidistant") from P.
  * Step 2 (green): construct circles centered at A′ and B′ having radius _r_. P and Q will be the points of intersection of these two circles.


Point Q is then the reflection of point P through line AB.
## Properties
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Reflection_\(mathematics\)&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Properties")]
The [matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_\(mathematics\) "Matrix \(mathematics\)") for a reflection is [orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal_matrix "Orthogonal matrix") with [determinant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Determinant "Determinant") −1 and [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue "Eigenvalue") −1, 1, 1, ..., 1. The product of two such matrices is a special orthogonal matrix that represents a rotation. Every [rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_\(mathematics\) "Rotation \(mathematics\)") is the result of reflecting in an even number of reflections in hyperplanes through the origin, and every [improper rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Improper_rotation "Improper rotation") is the result of reflecting in an odd number. Thus reflections generate the [orthogonal group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal_group "Orthogonal group"), and this result is known as the [Cartan–Dieudonné theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartan%E2%80%93Dieudonn%C3%A9_theorem "Cartan–Dieudonné theorem").
Similarly the [Euclidean group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_group "Euclidean group"), which consists of all isometries of Euclidean space, is generated by reflections in affine hyperplanes. In general, a [group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_\(mathematics\) "Group \(mathematics\)") generated by reflections in affine hyperplanes is known as a [reflection group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflection_group "Reflection group"). The [finite groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_group "Finite group") generated in this way are examples of [Coxeter groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter_group "Coxeter group").

## Reflection through a hyperplane in _n_ dimensions
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Reflection_\(mathematics\)&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Reflection through a hyperplane in n dimensions")]
More generally, if  S {\displaystyle S} ![{\\displaystyle S}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4611d85173cd3b508e67077d4a1252c9c05abca2) is a linear subspace and  P S {\displaystyle P_{S}} ![{\\displaystyle P_{S}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7188d30a379f0c54c369bc68554cc038068dd339) is the orthogonal projection onto  S {\displaystyle S} ![{\\displaystyle S}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4611d85173cd3b508e67077d4a1252c9c05abca2), then reflection across  S {\displaystyle S} ![{\\displaystyle S}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4611d85173cd3b508e67077d4a1252c9c05abca2) is given by      R S = 2 P S − I . {\displaystyle R_{S}=2P_{S}-I.} ![{\\displaystyle R_{S}=2P_{S}-I.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/8e8fe43f905c8da5a1abbcf8ea38a67087d26a70) [![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/dc/Reflection_across_a_plane_in_R3_using_a_projection_matrix.gif/500px-Reflection_across_a_plane_in_R3_using_a_projection_matrix.gif?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Reflection_across_a_plane_in_R3_using_a_projection_matrix.gif)Reflection across a plane in ℝ³ using an orthogonal projection matrix. Here  P = U ( U T U ) − 1 U T {\displaystyle P=U(U^{T}U)^{-1}U^{T}} ![{\\displaystyle P=U\(U^{T}U\)^{-1}U^{T}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ff3a280b469c487836ca73260611f5bdf4bc7df2) projects onto  col ⁡ ( U ) {\displaystyle \operatorname {col} (U)} ![{\\displaystyle \\operatorname {col} \(U\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/089e2ebe7a81de8be0ad7d9d228e7d5b0dab39b8), and  R = 2 P − I {\displaystyle R=2P-I} ![{\\displaystyle R=2P-I}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/292dd658b16ab58e842504b4f0bf776c325a14fe) is the corresponding reflection.
This fixes every vector in  S {\displaystyle S} ![{\\displaystyle S}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4611d85173cd3b508e67077d4a1252c9c05abca2) and negates every vector in  S ⊥ {\displaystyle S^{\perp }} ![{\\displaystyle S^{\\perp }}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b2c2ec743361c1b7b7ac3c40552bb8cf3368ab36). Since      P S + P S ⊥ = I , {\displaystyle P_{S}+P_{S^{\perp }}=I,} ![{\\displaystyle P_{S}+P_{S^{\\perp }}=I,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4d1c38e8488ebd21a9c9a099dcc40f7bfa7f0e4a)
the same reflection may equivalently be written as      R S = I − 2 P S ⊥ . {\displaystyle R_{S}=I-2P_{S^{\perp }}.} ![{\\displaystyle R_{S}=I-2P_{S^{\\perp }}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4a78d373d2ad9b16faf0a23b4a1787af59ec6a5b)
  
Thus a reflection can be described either by the subspace that remains fixed or by its orthogonal complement, whose vectors are reversed. In the preceding two-dimensional section,  l {\displaystyle l} ![{\\displaystyle l}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/829091f745070b9eb97a80244129025440a1cfac) spans the subspace that remains fixed; in the formula below,  a {\displaystyle a} ![{\\displaystyle a}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ffd2487510aa438433a2579450ab2b3d557e5edc) spans its orthogonal complement, whose vectors are reversed.
Given a vector  v {\displaystyle v} ![{\\displaystyle v}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e07b00e7fc0847fbd16391c778d65bc25c452597) in [Euclidean space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_space "Euclidean space") R n {\displaystyle \mathbb {R} ^{n}} ![{\\displaystyle \\mathbb {R} ^{n}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c510b63578322050121fe966f2e5770bea43308d), the formula for the reflection in the [hyperplane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperplane "Hyperplane") through the origin, [orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal "Orthogonal") to  a {\displaystyle a} ![{\\displaystyle a}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ffd2487510aa438433a2579450ab2b3d557e5edc), is given by      Ref a ⁡ ( v ) = v − 2 v ⋅ a a ⋅ a a , {\displaystyle \operatorname {Ref} _{a}(v)=v-2{\frac {v\cdot a}{a\cdot a}}a,} ![{\\displaystyle \\operatorname {Ref} _{a}\(v\)=v-2{\\frac {v\\cdot a}{a\\cdot a}}a,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c23921e8eb7259dcad8e78eecc76cbb77501cff1)
where  v ⋅ a {\displaystyle v\cdot a} ![{\\displaystyle v\\cdot a}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/0e2ef8f805cef6b84e3874e0b4c44bd0a01eaa12) denotes the [dot product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dot_product "Dot product") of  v {\displaystyle v} ![{\\displaystyle v}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e07b00e7fc0847fbd16391c778d65bc25c452597) with  a {\displaystyle a} ![{\\displaystyle a}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ffd2487510aa438433a2579450ab2b3d557e5edc). Note that the second term in the above equation is just twice the [vector projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_projection "Vector projection") of  v {\displaystyle v} ![{\\displaystyle v}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e07b00e7fc0847fbd16391c778d65bc25c452597) onto  a {\displaystyle a} ![{\\displaystyle a}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ffd2487510aa438433a2579450ab2b3d557e5edc). One can easily check that
  * Ref _a_(_v_) = − _v_ , if  v {\displaystyle v} ![{\\displaystyle v}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e07b00e7fc0847fbd16391c778d65bc25c452597) is parallel to  a {\displaystyle a} ![{\\displaystyle a}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ffd2487510aa438433a2579450ab2b3d557e5edc), and
  * Ref _a_(_v_) = _v_ , if  v {\displaystyle v} ![{\\displaystyle v}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e07b00e7fc0847fbd16391c778d65bc25c452597) is perpendicular to _a_.


Using the [geometric product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_product "Geometric product"), the formula is      Ref a ⁡ ( v ) = − a v a a 2 . {\displaystyle \operatorname {Ref} _{a}(v)=-{\frac {ava}{a^{2}}}.} ![{\\displaystyle \\operatorname {Ref} _{a}\(v\)=-{\\frac {ava}{a^{2}}}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ac7ec26e62f218c312da6382c2b864da7c39aa77)
Since these reflections are isometries of Euclidean space fixing the origin they may be represented by [orthogonal matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal_matrices "Orthogonal matrices"). The orthogonal matrix corresponding to the above reflection is the [matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_\(mathematics\) "Matrix \(mathematics\)")      R = I − 2 a a T a T a , {\displaystyle R=I-2{\frac {aa^{T}}{a^{T}a}},} ![{\\displaystyle R=I-2{\\frac {aa^{T}}{a^{T}a}},}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/15afa1f51a887c05c7a38054314051f4cbc05437)
where  I {\displaystyle I} ![{\\displaystyle I}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/535ea7fc4134a31cbe2251d9d3511374bc41be9f) denotes the  n × n {\displaystyle n\times n} ![{\\displaystyle n\\times n}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/59d2b4cb72e304526cf5b5887147729ea259da78) [identity matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_matrix "Identity matrix") and  a T {\displaystyle a^{T}} ![{\\displaystyle a^{T}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e60016a20ce32487efc7b8cda83fe316ea75b9fb) is the [transpose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transpose "Transpose") of a. Its entries are      R i j = δ i j − 2 a i a j ‖ a ‖ 2 , {\displaystyle R_{ij}=\delta _{ij}-2{\frac {a_{i}a_{j}}{\left\|a\right\|^{2}}},} ![{\\displaystyle R_{ij}=\\delta _{ij}-2{\\frac {a_{i}a_{j}}{\\left\\|a\\right\\|^{2}}},}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/3eab23a0f5e4e60281193ab50edf634530e05c25)
where _δ_ _ij_ is the [Kronecker delta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kronecker_delta "Kronecker delta").
The formula for the reflection in the affine hyperplane  v ⋅ a = c {\displaystyle v\cdot a=c} ![{\\displaystyle v\\cdot a=c}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/153ae5b4c99aa71dfb09e8562194f691d6c0bbcf) not through the origin is      Ref a , c ⁡ ( v ) = v − 2 v ⋅ a − c a ⋅ a a . {\displaystyle \operatorname {Ref} _{a,c}(v)=v-2{\frac {v\cdot a-c}{a\cdot a}}a.} ![{\\displaystyle \\operatorname {Ref} _{a,c}\(v\)=v-2{\\frac {v\\cdot a-c}{a\\cdot a}}a.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/2ff75485900d89e8333c70704affec106875dbfe)
